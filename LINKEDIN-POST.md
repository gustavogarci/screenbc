# LinkedIn Post — Hackathon Win Announcement

## Option A — Full Story

---

We won the UVic Health Innovation Hackathon with ScreenBC.

The problem: over 700,000 British Columbians don't have a family doctor. That means nobody is ordering their routine blood work. Nobody is catching their diabetes or high cholesterol before it becomes a $15,000 ER visit.

An HbA1c test costs $8. A lipid panel costs about the same. The tests are available at any LifeLabs. The clinical guidelines are well-established. The infrastructure exists. But without a doctor to order the test, it never happens.

BC already solved this for cancer — BC Cancer Screening proactively reaches out when you're due for a mammogram. No one waits for a family doctor to order that.

ScreenBC does the same thing for chronic disease. The system identifies patients overdue for screening, sends them a notification, gives them a lab requisition they can take to any LifeLabs, and interprets results using AI grounded in Canadian clinical guidelines — all without needing a physician in the loop.

Built by Gustavo Garcia in collaboration with Dr. Sally Carver, Family Physician. We evaluated 30+ ideas with input from multiple physicians and kept coming back to the same insight: the system isn't overwhelmed because people can't be treated — it's overwhelmed because nobody is screening them.

## Optional "Why We Won" Section

(Can be appended to either option above)

---

Why we won, in the judges' words: the idea is grounded in real clinical research, it's feasible and actionable today, and it focuses on preventive care — targeting a root cause of stress in the healthcare system, not just a symptom.

The model is already proven (BC Cancer Screening does exactly this for mammograms). The tests are cheap. The guidelines exist. The labs are everywhere. It just hasn't been done for chronic disease yet.

We evaluated over 30 ideas with multiple physicians. This one stood out because the infrastructure is already there, there's a clear path to implementation, and it doesn't require physicians to learn anything new or patients to change their behavior. It's a low-hanging fruit with an outsized impact.
